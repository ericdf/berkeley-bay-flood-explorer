King tide field guide: west Berkeley, winter 2026–27

On the morning of Thursday, December 24, 2026, the highest tide of this winter reaches about 7.4 ft NAVD88 (heights are explained below). This guide asks you to visit four spots in west Berkeley at that high tide, take photos and fill in a short sheet. Nobody has yet checked the Flood Explorer's map against what water actually does on the ground, and your visit is that check. You need no background: everything you need is on this page, and the sheet at the end prints on one page.

Two words first

King tide
The informal name for the highest tides of the year, which come a few days each winter when the Sun and Moon line up. They are predicted years ahead, so we know the dates and times now. A king tide is a normal tide, only higher: it shows today what an ordinary high tide may look like with a foot or so of sea level rise.
Feet NAVD88
Every height on this page, for water and for ground, is measured from the same fixed reference line that surveyors use (the North American Vertical Datum of 1988). A tide of 7.4 ft NAVD88 reaches any ground lower than 7.4 ft NAVD88 if the water has a path to it. Tide apps use a different reference (mean lower low water); at Richmond the two differ by a quarter foot or less, so an app's number will be close to the ones here.

  • Be at your first stop 45 minutes before high tide and plan to finish about 30 minutes after it. The water rises slowly near the peak, so the hour around high tide is all near the top, and that window also covers the small difference in timing between the Richmond gauge and Berkeley.
  • One visit on December 24 is the core of the job. Any other date in the table adds a second look; the November dates are a good rehearsal, about 0.3 ft lower.
  • Weather changes the water. These predictions leave weather out. Low air pressure and a south wind in a storm can push the Bay higher than predicted, and rain fills the storm drains. A stormy king tide is the most informative day to record, but only from a safe spot (see Safety).

The four stops

All four are a short drive apart, in the order given; the whole loop takes about an hour. Each stop says what the Flood Explorer's map expects at this tide. The most useful thing you can report is a stop where what you see disagrees with that, in either direction.

1. Bolivar Drive beside Aquatic Park

Where: Bolivar Drive along the west shore of Aquatic Park, near 88 Bolivar Dr, about 250 m south of where Addison Street reaches Bolivar Drive. Map (37.8634, −122.3009).

Why here: In the explorer's elevation data, this stretch of Bolivar Drive is the lowest street ground in Berkeley, about 7.0 to 7.2 ft NAVD88: a few inches below the December 24 tide. In the model the Bay cannot flow over the land to reach it; it could reach it only by coming up through the storm drains.

What the explorer shows at 7.4 ft NAVD88: no Bay water over the ground; the street is colored teal for "low ground that can fill through the storm drains", which assumes the drains let the Bay in. Whether they do is unknown, and that is what this stop tests.

Look for and photograph:

  • Any water standing on the road or in the gutter, and how far it spreads.
  • The storm drain grates in the gutter: dry, water standing in them, or water coming up out of them.
  • The lagoon: how close the water is to the top of its bank or to the path. Take a wide photo with something fixed in it (a light pole, a sign, a bench).

2. The frontage road at the south end of Aquatic Park

Where: the road between Aquatic Park and I-80 near Ashby Avenue, at the south end of the park. Map (37.8516, −122.2965). View it from the park side; do not stand in the road.

Why here: the ground here is about 7.6 to 7.8 ft NAVD88, a little above the December 24 tide. It is the next spot to go under if the water runs higher than predicted.

What the explorer shows at 7.4 ft NAVD88: dry. It shows this road filling through the drains only from about 8.4 ft NAVD88, a foot higher.

Look for and photograph: any water on the road or in the gutters and grates. If it is dry, a photo of the dry gutter and grate is still a useful result.

3. Berkeley Marina at University Avenue and Marina Boulevard

Where: the west end of University Avenue where it meets Marina Boulevard, near 285 Marina Blvd and the DoubleTree hotel. Map (37.8660, −122.3116).

Why here: the Marina is the most exposed part of Berkeley's shoreline, and a City study in 2019 found Marina Boulevard at risk by about 2050. The lowest street ground here is about 9.9 ft NAVD88, so the model expects the water to stay roughly 2.5 feet below the street today.

What the explorer shows at 7.4 ft NAVD88: dry streets and parking.

Look for and photograph:

  • The water against the harbor edge (rocks, wall or dock), with something fixed in the picture, such as a railing post or a bollard.
  • Your best guess of how many feet the water is below the top of that edge.
  • Any water on paths, lawns or parking lots, and any waves splashing over the edge.

4. Second Street at Gilman Street, beside the City transfer station

Where: Second Street from Gilman Street north past Harrison Street, along the City's transfer station and recycling center. Map of Second and Gilman (37.8785, −122.3058). The lowest point of the street in the explorer's data is north of Harrison Street: map (37.8819, −122.3069). Watch for garbage trucks; stay on the sidewalk.

Why here: the transfer station handles Berkeley's garbage, recycling and compost on their way to landfills and processors, plus hazardous items pulled from the loads. The City's environmental report on rebuilding it says Second Street already floods in heavy rain, because the Gilman Street storm drain and a creek culvert under I-80 are too small. The street's lowest ground is about 9.1 to 9.3 ft NAVD88.

What the explorer shows at 7.4 ft NAVD88: dry.

Look for and photograph: water at the storm drain grates and in the gutters, especially if it has rained in the last day. Note whether it is raining.

How to take useful photos

  1. Turn on your phone's location for the camera, so each photo records where it was taken.
  2. At each stop take one wide photo and one close-up. The wide photo shows the place; the close-up shows the water line.
  3. Put something fixed in every photo: a curb, a pole, a sign, a step. Water levels can only be compared against things that do not move.
  4. Note the stop and the time on the sheet, with the photo numbers if your phone shows them.
  5. If you can, go back to the same spots on an ordinary day at any time and take the same photos. A before-and-after pair is the most persuasive picture there is.
  6. Keep the original files. Do not crop or edit them.

Where the photos go

  1. The State's King Tides Project. The California Coastal Commission collects king tide photos from across the state on a public map. Upload them with its photo upload form, from a phone or a computer; its how-to-participate page explains the steps.
  2. The Flood Explorer. Send the original photos and the filled-in sheet to Eric Friedman, who maintains the explorer, in whatever way he arranged with you. He will compare them with the water level NOAA measured at Richmond that morning and with the explorer's map, and record the result, credited or anonymous as you prefer, in the explorer's source notes.

Safety

  • Stay on sidewalks, paths and parking areas. Do not walk on the rocks along the shore; they are slippery and waves can knock you down.
  • Do not wade or drive through standing water. It can hide holes and carry sewage and chemicals.
  • The roads beside I-80 carry fast traffic and trucks. Do not stand in the road for a photo.
  • In a storm, take photos from inside a parked car or from well back from the water, or skip the stop. No photo is worth a fall.
  • Go with someone if you can, and tell someone your route.

If you want to see the explorer's map

Open the Berkeley Bay Flood Explorer and pick "This winter's highest tide (Dec 24, 2026)" under "Tides and storm tides today". Zoom in on Aquatic Park and the Marina with the + button. Blue means under Bay water; teal means low ground that could fill through the storm drains; orange means low ground with no path for the water. The explorer is an educational comparison on bare-earth elevation data, not a flood forecast, and this visit is a check on it.
