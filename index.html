Berkeley Bay Flood Explorer

Explore how storm tides, tsunamis, sea level rise and heavy rain could affect west Berkeley's streets, critical infrastructure, I-80, pollution sources and the Bay shoreline. Not a flood forecast.

Explain this

Water can come in four ways: over the shoreline when the Bay rises; up the storm drains, when the Bay is higher than the pipes' outlets and nothing closes them; up from below, as groundwater rises with the Bay; and in a tsunami.

Water also has to get out. When the drain outlets are under the tide, rain that falls in Berkeley cannot drain and pools in the low streets. And water going out, as a flood drains back or a tsunami pulls back, can carry what it picked up, from contaminated ground and stored chemicals, into the Bay.

These can happen together: a winter storm brings a high storm tide and heavy rain at once, and both come on top of the rise in sea level. The explorer shows Bay water and storm drains at a level you choose, can add a rainstorm, and outlines the State's tsunami evacuation area. It does not model waves, groundwater or a tsunami's flooding.

Explain this

NAVD88 is the zero that land and water heights are measured from (the North American Vertical Datum of 1988). An ordinary high tide off Berkeley is 6.2 ft on it.

Still water is the Bay's level without waves. Waves add height on the open Berkeley shoreline.

"At today's sea level" means with the sea where it is now, not today's weather. A "100-year" storm tide has a 1% chance of happening in any year.

Explain this

USGS CoSMoS v2.1 (San Francisco Bay, 2014) models tides, storms with waves, ocean swell, river flow and sea level rise together, so its water includes waves and is not still water. Each map is a sea level rise plus a storm, chosen below, and does not follow your level. The outline comes from CoSMoS's flood depth rasters; the extent polygons on the City's and Our Coast, Our Future's maps cover more land (SOURCE_NOTES C21, H1). Not a flood forecast.

Why these maps disagree

The explorer and CoSMoS are different models. The explorer is a still-water fill: any ground below the level that connects to the Bay is wet (a model rule). CoSMoS models a total water level, with tides, storm surge, in-Bay waves, ocean swell and river flow, so its water is not a still-water level. Where they differ, which one is closer to what would happen is not established; neither has been checked here against observed flooding.

  • Low agreement means the models differ. At 0 to 0.75 m of rise the best agreement is 13 to 45% with the 100-year storm, where CoSMoS floods 1.4 to 14 ha of Berkeley land and keeps dry the North Basin shoreline strip and the west edge of Aquatic Park, which the fill wets; with the background tide alone it is 6 to 18%, on 0.5 to 2.2 ha. On the Marina the two agree closely (SOURCE_NOTES C21).
  • Behind I-80. CoSMoS floods none of the transfer station, Second St, Bolivar Dr or Eastshore Hwy in any scenario up to 2.0 m, although its water surface off Berkeley reaches 13.82 to 14.21 ft NAVD88, above the 13.35 ft NAVD88 at which the fill crosses (C6). How CoSMoS represents the freeway embankment is not documented, so this does not settle the question; Caltrans road profiles would (C21, F2).
  • The CoSMoS files differ among themselves. Its extent polygons, on the City's and Our Coast, Our Future's maps, cover more land than its depth rasters, which this outline is drawn from; its 20-year storm floods more Berkeley land than its 100-year storm at 0.75 to 2.0 m of rise (C21).
  • Position. The CoSMoS outline is moved from NAD83 to WGS 84 as the ART layers are, about 1.5 m (C1, C29). On this grid the move changes no cell of it.
  • The best-matching level is a comparison only. It is the still-water level at which the explorer's map looks most like the CoSMoS map. It is not validation, and it is not an equivalent scenario: the explorer at that level does not represent the CoSMoS storm, whose water includes waves.

A king tide field check this winter (E10), the City's records on outfalls and tide gates (D6) and Caltrans profiles of I-80 are the evidence that could test the differences.
